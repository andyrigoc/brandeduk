Workwear Embroidery and Logo Embroidery Explained

What happens between uploading a logo and receiving finished embroidered workwear.
By Branded UK Production Team · 22 September 2026 · Reviewed by Anderson Ricotta
IMAGE REQUIRED

Learn how workwear embroidery is produced, why logos need digitising and which garments and designs suit embroidered branding.

What workwear embroidery actually involves

Workwear embroidery uses coloured thread to build a logo directly into the garment. The result has texture, depth and a professional appearance that suits uniforms worn repeatedly. Logo embroidery is commonly used on polo shirts, sweatshirts, fleeces, jackets, caps, aprons and many other stable fabrics.

The machine cannot sew directly from a JPEG or standard logo file. The design must first be converted into stitch instructions. This process is called digitising and controls stitch type, direction, density, underlay and sewing order.

Why digitising matters

A good embroidery file is designed around the fabric and the final size. The same artwork may require different settings for a polo shirt and a structured cap. Fine details can close up, small gaps can disappear and thin lettering may become difficult to read if the file is prepared without considering thread and fabric movement.

Digitising is not a visual filter. It is production engineering. A skilled operator decides where stitches begin and end, which areas need support and how the design should run efficiently without losing its identity.

Artwork that embroiders well

Bold shapes, clear lettering and compact logos normally produce reliable embroidered workwear. Very small text, photographic shading, distressed textures and fine gradients may need simplification. This is not a reduction in quality; it is an adjustment from flat artwork to a physical thread construction.

Send the original vector artwork where possible. If it is unavailable, provide the largest clean image you have. Avoid screenshots and logos copied from a website because they often contain blurred edges and incorrect colours.

Garments and positions

Left chest logo embroidery is the standard choice for professional uniforms. Right chest can carry a name, role or secondary mark. Sleeves work well for compact flags or accreditations. Caps require specific consideration because seams, crown shape and frames affect the sewing area.

Large dense back logos are possible in some cases but are often better printed. They can become heavy, stiff and expensive in thread. The garment and position should be assessed before the method is confirmed.

Checking the finished result

Approval should cover logo version, colours, position and size. Screen colours are not identical to physical thread, so brand references should be supplied when colour accuracy matters. Production checks should include spelling, alignment, loose threads, fabric stability and overall appearance.
